History of Media ↓

  • 15th Century

    Johannes Gutenberg’s invention of the movable type printing press made mass production of print media possible, helping usher in massive cultural movements like the European Renaissance and the Protestant Reformation.

  • 1800s

    In addition to the breakthroughs in audio broadcasting, inventors in the 1800s made significant advances in visual media.

  • 1810

    German printer Friedrich Koenig pushed media production even further when he hooked the steam engine up to a printing press, enabling the industrialization of printed media.

  • 1830s

    Major daily newspapers faced a new threat from the rise of penny papers—low-priced broadsheets serving sensational daily news of murder and adventure over dry political news.

  • 1888

    George Eastman developed the Kodak camera, anticipating that Americans would welcome an inexpensive, easy-to-use camera into their homes as they had with the radio and telephone.

  • 1905

    Moving pictures were first seen around the turn of the century, with the first U.S. projection-hall opening in Pittsburgh in 1905.

  • 1920s

    Radios were less expensive than telephones and widely available by the 1920s, allowing huge numbers of people to listen to the same event at the same time. Did radio fuel the consumerist boom of the 1920s, or did it become popular because it appealed to a society already exploring consumerist tendencies? Probably a little bit of both.

  • 1920s - 1930s

    Hollywood had already created its first stars, most notably Charlie Chaplin; by the end of the 1930s, Americans were watching color films with full sound.

  • 1924

    Calvin Coolidge’s preelection speech reached more than 20 million people. Radio was a boon for advertisers, who now had access to a large and captive audience.

  • 1946

    About 17,000 televisions existed in the United States.

  • 1960s

    Media theorist Marshall McLuhan famously coined the phrase “the medium is the message (McLuhan, 1964),” meaning that every medium delivers information differently and content is shaped by its medium of transmission.

  • 1969

    Management consultant Peter Drucker predicted that the next major technological innovation would be an electronic appliance that would revolutionize the way people lived just as thoroughly as Thomas Edison’s light bulb had.

  • 1980s & 1990s

    The media world faced drastic changes once again with the spread of cable television.

  • 1994

    “Each medium has a special way of representing ideas that emphasize particular ways of thinking and de-emphasize others (Kay, 1994).” Written when the Internet was just transitioning to an open public system.

  • 1996

    The Telecommunications Act of 1996 attempted to foster competition by deregulating the industry, but resulted in mergers that left broadcast control in the hands of a few large corporations.

  • 1997

    Treating television as evil is just as reductive and silly as treating it like a toaster with pictures (Wallace, 1997).

  • Late 1990s

    Until the advent of digital video recorders in the late 1990s, it was impossible to pause and rewind a live television broadcast.
